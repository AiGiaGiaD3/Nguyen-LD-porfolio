How I use AI to design training

AI did not replace my design judgement. It removed the gap between a design decision and something a learner can hold in their hand.

By Võ Trung Nguyên · Training Design & Development · November 2026

For most of my career, the expensive part of training design was not the thinking. It was the building. I could see the activity I wanted: a matching game for policy rules, a practice deck for recruiting conversations, a card that turns a good intention into a weekly plan. Then I would write a brief, wait for a vendor or an eLearning slot, and accept whatever survived the budget. Most ideas ended as a slide with a table on it.

Over the last two years that changed. I now design, build and test interactive learning tools myself, usually within a few days, and deploy them as single web pages that learners open from a QR code on their phones. The four demos on this site were built that way. This essay explains the process behind them, what AI does in it, and what I deliberately keep for myself.

The principle: AI builds, I decide

I use AI in the middle of the process, not at the start or the end. The start is a performance problem, which needs a person who knows the learners and the business. The end is a judgement about accuracy, compliance and tone, which needs someone accountable. Between those two points there is a lot of drafting, building and reworking, and that is where AI earns its place.

1. DiagnoseWhat should people do differently, and what stops them? I own this.
2. SpecifyOutcomes, activity, feedback rules and constraints, written as a brief. I own this.
3. BuildContent drafts, scripts and the working tool, produced with AI against my standards.
4. VerifyFact, compliance and classroom checks before anything reaches a learner. I own this.

Step 1: Start from the behaviour, not the content

Every tool starts with one question: what will the learner do in the field that they cannot do today? For the Policy Match game the answer was not "know the policy terms". New frontline staff had read them. The gap was recognition: hearing a customer say "I missed a payment, am I still covered?" and knowing which provision applies. That single sentence decided the whole design. Situations are written in the customer's words, all eight rule cards stay on screen, and feedback ends with a line they can actually say.

I write this down before I open any AI tool, using the frameworks I have used for years: Bloom's taxonomy for the level of the outcome, 70-20-10 for where the learning will really happen, and MEDIC (motivation, explanation, demonstration, imitation, consolidation) for the flow of the session. AI is good at producing activities. It is not good at knowing which activity matters for these learners. If I skip this step I get a polished tool that solves the wrong problem.

Step 2: Turn my standards into reusable AI skills

The biggest change in my practice was not a better prompt. It was writing my design standards down once, as structured instruction files that the AI follows every time. I maintain several of these "skills", each the equivalent of a senior designer's checklist:

  • A learning-game designer skill that encodes how I build tools: single-file HTML, mobile first, large touch targets, feedback after every answer, no login, no data collection, a result the learner can save and share, and the brand rules of whichever company the tool is for.
  • A facilitation-script skill that writes trainer guides in the MEDIC structure and in the exact column format our trainers use, so drafts arrive ready to review instead of ready to reformat.
  • A study-material skill for exam-review content in the Cornell format, built after learners told us earlier review packs were hard to scan on paper.

This is what makes AI output consistent. A one-off prompt produces one good result and nine different ones. A skill produces the same quality bar on the tenth tool as on the first, and a colleague can use it without knowing how I think. In team terms, it turns personal know-how into a shared asset.

A good prompt helps me once. A written standard helps the whole team every time.

Step 3: Prototype the real thing, then test it in the room

Because building is fast, I no longer present storyboards for approval. I present a working tool. Stakeholders tap through it on their own phones, and the conversation moves from "do you like the concept?" to "this explanation is too long" and "managers will skip this step". Those comments are concrete, so the next version is better.

Each tool then goes into a real session before it is called finished. Classroom testing finds things no review does. On a phone the confirm button has to sit at thumb height. A 10-minute timer has to be visible to the trainer. Vietnamese text breaks badly on narrow screens unless the last two words of a line are kept together. Each of these became a rule in my skill file, so the next tool starts from the improved version.

Step 4: Keep the judgement human

In life insurance a training tool can do harm. A salesperson who repeats a wrong rule to a customer creates a complaint. A team leader who over-promises income creates a resignation six months later. So there is a hard gate between what AI drafts and what learners see, and I sit at that gate.

  • Every fact is traced to a source. In Policy Match each feedback panel cites the clause it comes from, and notes where a sample script mentions an operational channel that the contract itself does not.
  • Guardrails are part of the content. Each of the 30 cards in the Candidate Concerns Coach says what the manager must not say and which official document backs the answer. AI drafts persuasive language easily. My job is to make it honest.
  • Tone is checked by a person. I read every line a learner will say out loud. If it sounds like a script, a customer will hear it as one.

AI also helps me with this step. I ask it to attack its own drafts: find the claims without a source, list the places a learner could misread the instruction, play a sceptical compliance reviewer. That does not replace review. It makes my review faster and more thorough.

What this changes for a training function

BeforeNow
Interactive tools were a vendor project, so most sessions used slides and paper.Interactive practice is the default and costs designer time, not budget.
Quality depended on who designed the module.Written skill files keep the same standard across designers and programs.
Feedback was given verbally, if at all, and disappeared after class.Tools give structured feedback and produce an image or PDF the learner sends to their manager.
Changes after a pilot meant a new vendor brief.Changes after a pilot are made the same week.
Tools needed apps, logins or an LMS slot.A QR code opens a page that works on any phone, with no learner data collected.

The last row matters to HR and IT as much as to L&D. The tools are static pages. They collect no personal data, need no integration and can be hosted on any approved web space. When a tool needs to change, I change one file.

Bridging the classroom and the field

The part of this work I care about most is what happens after the session. Two of the demos end by handing evidence to the line manager: the 90-Day Commitment Card with a weekly activity plan, and the pair-practice summary from the Candidate Concerns Coach with a step-by-step score. The manager now has something specific to coach against. That is how a training event connects to the 70 per cent of learning that happens on the job.

The next step is to use AI inside that reinforcement loop as well: role-play partners that push back like a real candidate, short practice tasks targeted at a learner's weak steps, and reflection prompts after a real customer meeting. I am designing these with the same rule as everything else on this site. AI builds and responds; a person decides what good looks like.

What I would tell another L&D team

  1. Write your standards before you write prompts. The value is in the checklist, not in the wording of the request.
  2. Ship small, real tools. One 10-minute activity used in every onboarding class beats a large course nobody finishes.
  3. Test in the room. Real learners on real phones find problems that reviews never will.
  4. Never outsource accountability. AI can draft every word. A named person still signs off every fact.
